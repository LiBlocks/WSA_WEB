AutoLand Engineering

WSA's own autonomous landing system for returning Aegis boosters to a chosen landing site. It is written in kOS (KerboScript) and developed in-house rather than relying on MechJeb's landing automation.

Demonstrated

Target-pad landing. AutoLand V2.3 has landed an Aegis booster directly on its designated pad.

In development

Repeatability. More testing and refinement of V2.3's precision, on the way to V3.0.

Planned

V3.0. A mature, dependable system for routine booster recovery.

Latest achievement · AutoLand V2.3

AutoLand V2.3: First Successful Target-Pad Landing

After working through powered-glide guidance, trajectory prediction, landing-target correction and Kerbin's rotation, WSA AutoLand V2.3 brought an Aegis booster down directly on its designated landing pad.

That was AutoLand's original objective, and it is now demonstrated. It came in V2.3, sooner than the roughly six versions first expected.

A new booster: the booster in the video is the new Aegis Mk1.7 design. It adds a hinge for WSA's future booster catch, and more about the catch will be released soon.

What comes next: making the landing repeatable and dependable, so it is ready for WSA's growing reusable-booster programme.

The video shows one landing. No telemetry was recorded on screen, so its touchdown speed and exact distance from the pad's centre are not published here.

The Road to the Pad

How AutoLand went from a persistent miss in V2.2 to a landing on the pad in V2.3.

  1. V2.2: powered-glide guidance

    Earlier versions struggled with leftover horizontal speed, inaccurate touchdown prediction, large landing offsets, and combining horizontal correction with a gradual turn to a near-vertical landing attitude. V2.2 brought powered-glide guidance and landing-target correction together: instead of a straight vertical drop, the booster steers its horizontal motion while easing toward a near-vertical attitude.

    In an early test, the guidance brought the booster down close to another booster. That attempt ended in a tip-over, but it showed how far positioning had improved.

  2. V2.2: the missing rotation

    V2.2 kept missing its target by more than 1.6 km. A miss that large and that consistent made the team suspicious, and they realised the guidance was missing Kerbin's rotation: it was not separating velocity in the inertial frame from velocity relative to Kerbin's turning surface. While a booster falls, the ground beneath it keeps turning, so leaving the rotation out puts the predicted touchdown in the wrong place.

    With the rotation accounted for, V2.2 landed within about 500 m of its target.

  3. V2.3: on the pad

    V2.3 closed the remaining few hundred metres. Its first prototype touched down on the designated landing pad, though off-centre. The precision landing in the video then put the booster down on the pad's marked centre: AutoLand's first successful target-pad landings.

Development History

AutoLand grew its own descent control, powered guidance, trajectory prediction, target correction, diagnostics and vehicle awareness, one version at a time:

V1.6
Status: Last stable version before the A/B series

Could land a booster on water, but the booster could still tip over after touchdown.

B1
Status: Failed

An experimental build that failed in testing. The exact cause is not documented.

A1–A4
Status: Experimental

Successive builds exploring throttle smoothing and increasingly autonomous landing.

V2.0
Status: Adopted

The A4 build became V2.0, the start of the V2 line.

V2.1
Status: Superseded

Added entry-burn control, a drag model and in-flight diagnostics, aiming the booster at its landing area during reentry.

V2.2
Status: Stable

Powered-glide guidance with landing prediction and landing-target correction. It missed by more than 1.6 km until Kerbin's rotation was accounted for; after that it landed within about 500 m of its target.

V2.3
Status: In testing: target-pad landing demonstrated

Closed the last few hundred metres and landed on the target pad, first off-centre and then on the pad's marked centre. More testing comes next, on the way to V3.0.

What AutoLand Does Today

These capabilities are already developed. The work ahead is to verify and refine them as a whole system, not to add them.

Adaptive guidance

Guidance responds to changing flight conditions and to what the vehicle can actually do.

Predictive landing and trajectory planning

A drag model predicts where and how the booster will touch down, and the guidance corrects toward the target from that prediction.

Powered glide and target correction

Steers horizontal motion during the powered descent while easing toward a near-vertical landing attitude, guided by the landing error in latitude and longitude.

Kerbin-rotation awareness

The predictor accounts for Kerbin's rotation, keeping inertial and surface-relative velocity apart.

Vehicle configuration awareness

Accounts for different vehicle configurations, using the thrust actually available from the active engines and the vehicle's current mass.

Autonomous diagnostics

Monitors its own performance during the descent and can flag abnormal behaviour.

Roadmap

AutoLand has shown that a target-pad landing is possible. Next it has to show the landing can be repeated reliably under different conditions. V3.0 is meant to be that mature, dependable system, not the first version able to land on the pad.

Next phase: precision and repeatability

▼
  • Repeatability across many landing attempts.
  • Precision from different starting trajectories and flight conditions.
  • Reliable performance across different Aegis configurations.
  • Robustness against unusual or unfavourable descents.
  • Checking predicted touchdowns against actual landing results.

Some of the capabilities behind these already exist; the work is to verify, refine and validate the whole system. The exact V3.0 acceptance criteria have not been finalised.

Possible future: landing-site intelligence

▼
  • Choosing custom landing sites.
  • Primary and backup recovery locations.
  • Awareness of different landing-site characteristics.
  • More flexible choice of recovery site.

These are possible future capabilities, not finished features.

Related projects, separate from AutoLand

▼
  • Aegis flight automation (concept): automating more of an Aegis flight, such as ascent, staging, boostback burns and custom recovery locations. AutoLand's job is the powered descent and landing.
  • Booster catch (in development): a mechanical structure to catch and secure returning Aegis boosters instead of landing them on legs or recovering them from the sea. After considering a capture ring and cable systems, WSA chose to pursue an original chopstick-style design. The new Aegis Mk1.7 in the video adds a hinge for the catch, and more about it will be released soon. A catch has not been demonstrated yet: the pad landing above was a landing on legs.

Why AutoLand Matters to WSA

Reusability

A booster is only truly reusable if it can come back to a chosen place, again and again. Landing on the pad was the first step; doing it reliably is the next.

Engineering Learning

Building AutoLand meant understanding guidance, drag, thrust prediction and reference frames, down to how Kerbin's rotation moves a falling booster.

Independence

AutoLand is WSA's own software, not a stock KSP feature or an external autopilot, so WSA understands and controls every part of it.
