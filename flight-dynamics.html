Formula and assumptions

r = R + h

v = √(μ / r)

T = 2π √(r³ / μ)

  • μ is the body's standard gravitational parameter (GM), in m³/s².
  • r is the distance from the body's centre, not the altitude: the altitude h is converted by adding the reference radius R.
  • An ideal circular orbit round a single point mass: no atmosphere, no other bodies, no oblateness. Below the top of an atmosphere, a real orbit decays.

Formula and assumptions

vescape = √(2μ / r)

  • The ideal speed at distance r from the centre that carries a coasting object away from the body for good, in a two-body model with no further thrust once that speed is reached.
  • It is not the speed a rocket must reach at the surface to launch: a rocket keeps thrusting as it climbs, and gravity, drag and steering losses change what it actually needs.
  • No atmosphere and no other bodies (escaping Kerbin still leaves you in orbit round Kerbol).

Formula and assumptions

at = (r1 + r2) / 2

vc = √(μ / r)    vt = √(μ (2/r − 1/at))

Δv1 = |vt1 − vc1|    Δv2 = |vc2 − vt2|    Δvtotal = Δv1 + Δv2

t = π √(at³ / μ)

  • Circular, coplanar initial and final orbits round the same body; two instantaneous burns; ideal two-body gravity.
  • Raising an orbit burns prograde twice; lowering burns retrograde twice. The burn sizes are the same either way.
  • Not included: plane changes and inclination differences, eccentric starting orbits, finite burn durations, atmospheres and third-body effects. For an interplanetary transfer round Kerbol, the burns are relative to Kerbol, not the ejection burn from a parking orbit.
  • The phase angle is how far ahead of you (negative: behind) a target in the final orbit must be at the first burn for the two of you to meet. It is a planning aid; check it against your own maneuver nodes.
  • Equations as set out in Orbital Mechanics & Astrodynamics: Hohmann transfer.

Formula and assumptions

TWR = Fthrust / (m × g)

a = Fthrust / m    Frequired = TWRtarget × m × g

  • Mass and thrust are converted to kg and N before calculating (1 t = 1,000 kg, 1 kN = 1,000 N).
  • A TWR above 1 means the thrust exceeds the weight, so the vehicle can lift off vertically from where g applies. Choosing a body uses its surface gravity μ/R².
  • In KSP, use the thrust of the engines that will actually be firing in this stage and configuration. Thrust also changes with altitude in an atmosphere; use the value for where you are.
  • The acceleration is from thrust alone, before gravity, drag and other forces. Mass falls as propellant burns, so TWR rises during a burn.

Formula and assumptions

Δv = Isp × g0 × ln(m0 / mf)

mf, target = m0 / eΔvtarget / (Isp g0)

  • m0 is the initial wet mass, mf the final dry or remaining mass, Isp the specific impulse in seconds and g0 = 9.80665 m/s².
  • The ideal Tsiolkovsky rocket equation for a single stage with one engine type. It leaves out gravity losses, atmospheric drag, steering losses and staging, so it is the most this stage could give, not a guaranteed flight figure.
  • For a multi-stage vehicle, work out each stage separately with the mass it carries and add the results.

These are educational and planning tools built on simplified models (two-body gravity, ideal impulsive burns, the ideal rocket equation). They are not certified flight-analysis software. Check important numbers against your own flight data.
