Aegis: Reusable Booster Family

Aegis is WSA's proven reusable booster family, developed from early recovery experiments to reliable, flight-tested hardware. The family includes multiple configurations optimized for different mission payloads and operational scenarios.

Development History

▼

Aegis evolved through multiple iterations as WSA tested recovery methods, refined booster structure, and improved landing guidance:

  • Early Experiments: Initial subscale tests of reusable-booster concepts and atmospheric-entry recovery methods.
  • First Full-Scale Tests: Multiple test flights with varying payloads and recovery approaches. First successful booster recovery achieved.
  • Landing Guidance Development: Autonomous landing algorithms refined through repeated flights. AutoLand guidance system matured across multiple versions.
  • Family Expansion: Development of multiple configurations (Quartet, Quintet, Nonet, Heavy, Super Heavy, Max) to support different mission profiles.
  • Operational Maturation: 47+ successful recoveries achieved. Booster family now transitioned toward crewed-certification readiness.
  • Advanced Recovery Concepts: Research into Mark II (side-booster) and Mark V (main-stage) recovery architectures for larger launch vehicles.

Configurations & Variants

▼

Aegis configurations are named for their engine count or capability tier:

Quartet (4-engine)
Smaller configuration. Used for subscale testing and lower-mass payloads.
Quintet (5-engine)
Mid-range configuration. Primary test and early-operational configuration.
Nonet (9-engine)
Higher-thrust variant for larger payloads and higher-velocity missions.
Heavy
Increased capacity. Designed for ambitious mission profiles and larger orbital infrastructure payloads.
Super Heavy
Largest Aegis variant. Used for Aegis-1 Super Heavy heavy-lift configuration and FAMMOK-3 planning.
Max
Experimental variant exploring maximum performance of the Aegis platform architecture.

Note: Configuration designations describe relative capability within the Aegis family. Precise performance specifications vary by test and mission profile. All configurations have demonstrated autonomous landing capability.

Landing & Recovery

▼

Aegis boosters use AutoLand autonomous guidance for reliable recovery:

  • Descent Prediction: AutoLand calculates whether current trajectory can achieve vertical descent with available thrust.
  • Throttle Control: Smooth throttle adjustments maintain controlled descent rate and landing envelope.
  • Landing Accuracy: Guidance targets designated landing zones. Typical landing errors range from on-target to several kilometers depending on conditions.
  • Touchdown Management: Final-approach phase aims for gentle vertical descent and manageable touchdown velocity.
  • Proven Performance: 47+ successful recoveries across varied landing sites, environmental conditions, and payload scenarios.

See the AutoLand Engineering page for detailed guidance system documentation.

Advanced Recovery Concepts

▼

WSA is exploring advanced multi-booster recovery architectures:

Mark II Direction (Side-Booster Recovery)

Concept for recovering side boosters on a multi-booster configuration. Involves coordinating separation, staging, and autonomous recovery of multiple vehicle segments.

Status: Demonstrated in concept; integration with full launch vehicle ongoing.
Mark V Direction (Main-Stage Recovery)

Architecture for recovering the main booster stage after upper-stage separation. Requires management of velocity reduction, attitude control, and autonomous targeting.

Status: Design phase; flight validation pending.
Aegis-1 Super Heavy

Heavy-lift configuration combining multiple Aegis-class boosters with single recoverable main stage. Intended for FAMMOK-3 deep-space rescue missions.

Status: Planning phase for FAMMOK-3 application.

Failures & Lessons

▼

Aegis development involved failures that drove important engineering improvements:

  • Early Recovery Challenges: Initial tests showed that surviving descent and landing required more than structural design—active control and prediction were essential.
  • Guidance Refinement: Early landing guidance produced inconsistent results. Throttle smoothing, descent prediction tuning, and altitude-management algorithms required iteration.
  • Post-Landing Stability: Boosters occasionally tipped over after landing, damaging fins or engines. This drove development of better landing-gear design and touchdown-velocity management.
  • Scaling Challenges: Moving from smaller to larger configurations revealed issues with fuel reserves, thrust-to-weight ratios, and guidance system margins.
  • Environmental Factors: Water landings, rough terrain, and variable wind conditions required more robust guidance and better sensor reliability.

Each failure led to documented improvements. The 47+ successful recoveries represent learning accumulated through test flights and operational missions.

Current Status & Next Steps

▼

Operational Status: Aegis family is mature and operationally ready for regular missions.

  • Recovery Capability: High reliability demonstrated across all tested configurations. Ready for frequent-flight operations.
  • Crewed Certification: Preparing for certification testing to support crewed missions to KGOSS and beyond.
  • Legacy Role: Aegis continues to serve existing mission requirements while Lyra development progresses for future high-capacity needs.
  • Research Platform: AutoLand development and advanced recovery concepts use Aegis as the test bed for new guidance and control algorithms.

Lyra: Next-Generation Booster Family

Lyra represents a new direction in WSA's launch-vehicle development. Built on lessons from Aegis, Lyra offers higher capacity, refined staging, and improved operational efficiency for supporting KGOSS expansion and more ambitious mission architectures.

Design Philosophy

▼

Lyra was designed to address lessons from Aegis development and requirements for future WSA missions:

  • Higher Payload Capacity: Exceeds Aegis payload envelope to support KGOSS construction, deep-space missions, and crewed operations.
  • Cleaner Architecture: Simplified staging and integration compared to some Aegis experimental variants.
  • Autonomous Recovery: Builds on proven AutoLand technology for booster recovery across all variants.
  • Operational Efficiency: Designed for rapid turnaround and frequent flights, reducing refurbishment time between missions.
  • Scalability: Three-tier architecture (Selene, Sol, Astra) provides mission flexibility while maintaining design commonality.

Three-Tier Architecture

▼

Lyra's capability is organized into three named tiers rather than simple variant lists:

Lyra Selene (Smaller Tier)

Entry-level variant optimized for rapid turnaround and frequent operations. Lower payload capacity supports small-satellite deployment, crew rotation for orbital stations, and subscale mission testing.

Development Status: Prototype integration underway (Lyra P-1E).
Lyra Sol (Medium Tier)

Primary operational configuration balancing payload capacity, operational cost, and mission flexibility. Designed as the workhorse for regular logistics, medium-mass payloads, and diverse mission profiles.

Development Status: Design phase; prototype construction to follow.
Lyra Astra (Larger Tier)

High-capacity variant for ambitious missions. Maximum payload envelope supports KGOSS module delivery, crewed deep-space missions, and scientific payloads. Designed for orbital infrastructure deployment.

Development Status: Early design phase; integration pending completion of smaller variants.

Note: All three tiers share common booster architecture, AutoLand guidance system, and recovery procedures. Scaling focuses on payload section, engine arrangement, and propellant capacity.

Prototype & Development Status

▼

Lyra P-1E: Prototype 1 Experimental—the current development vehicle for the Lyra family.

  • Status: Integration of primary systems underway. First flight testing planned.
  • Configuration: Experimental prototype with flexible payload bay for various test loads.
  • Role: Validation of Lyra booster architecture, staging separation, landing guidance, and autonomous-recovery procedures.
  • Expected Outcome: Successful first flight will clear path for Lyra Selene production and scaling.

Development Timeline: P-1E first flight is the critical path item for Lyra program. Completion allows qualification testing and transition to operational Selene, Sol, and Astra variants.

Multi-Engine Architecture Concepts

▼

Lyra development has explored various engine arrangements to optimize thrust, cost, and performance:

  • Main Stage: Multi-engine arrangement (up to 21 engines on main stage in some concepts) to achieve target thrust and provide redundancy.
  • Side Stages (Proposed): Additional booster segments for heavy-lift configurations. Concepts include up to 39 total engines across all stages.
  • Engine Commonality: All variants designed to use the same engine type to simplify manufacturing, logistics, and spare parts management.
  • Thrust Redundancy: Multi-engine design allows throttling or shutdown of individual engines if needed, improving robustness.

Note: Engine arrangements are design concepts undergoing validation. Final configurations will be determined through testing and operational requirements.

Role in WSA Future

▼

Lyra is essential to several WSA strategic goals:

  • KGOSS Construction: Higher payload capacity supports station modules, fuel tanks, and expansion capabilities beyond current assembly limits.
  • Deep-Space Missions: Enables ambitious FAMMOK-3 rescue and exploration missions requiring larger launch vehicles.
  • Crewed Spaceflight: Supports crewed missions to distant destinations and transport of heavy payloads requiring human supervision.
  • Next-Generation Infrastructure: Enables future orbital refueling stations, larger habitats, and advanced mission architectures requiring high-capacity launch.
  • Autonomous Flight Research: Lyra serves as a platform for advancing AutoLand and autonomous operations on more powerful, faster-flying vehicles.

Aircraft & Spaceplane Program

WSA operates experimental aircraft and spaceplane programs to develop flight-control concepts, test atmospheric aerodynamics, and explore reusable orbital transportation.

Swift-1X Test Aircraft

▼

Swift-1X is a stable, fast, small aircraft used for testing flight-control concepts, atmospheric handling, and aerodynamic behavior.

  • Role: Technology demonstrator for flight-control algorithms and atmospheric research.
  • Configuration: Flexible for crewed and uncrewed operation, allowing various sensor and payload arrangements.
  • Applications: Testing autopilot concepts, high-speed flight control, atmospheric-entry dynamics, and vehicle stability across speed regimes.
  • Status: Active test platform. Routine flight program ongoing.

SSTO Development

▼

Single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO) development explores whether a spaceplane can reach orbit without staging:

Mk2 Spaceplane-Based SSTO

A developing SSTO concept based on Mk2 spaceplane fuselage has evolved to approximately 80 tonnes after design iterations.

Atmospheric Phase:

  • Four engines (air-breathing) in expanded configuration
  • Two shock cone intakes and four radial intakes for air intake
  • Approximately 800 units monopropellant for RCS

Orbital Phase:

  • NERV engines for vacuum operation
  • Four crew modules for crewed missions
  • Docking port for orbital rendezvous

Development Challenges:

  • Fuel Depletion: Early atmospheric-to-orbital transition was difficult. Early designs exhausted liquid fuel near 25 km altitude, requiring incomplete ascent or staged recovery.
  • Orbital Achievement: Later designs using NERV engines reached approximately 200 km orbit, but without sufficient RCS propellant for docking maneuvers.
  • Docking Problem: Achieving orbit proved insufficient for mission success. The SSTO reached orbit but lacked RCS capability needed for rendezvous with KGOSS.

Key Lesson: Reaching orbit is not the same as completing a mission. Systems must be designed not just to achieve altitude, but to perform the full mission profile including rendezvous, docking, and return. SSTO designers must balance fuel for ascent, orbital maneuvers, and return—a difficult trade on a single stage.

Current Status: SSTO development continues as an experimental program. The fundamental trade-offs between atmospheric efficiency, orbital capability, and mission flexibility remain active research areas.
