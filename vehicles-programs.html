Aegis: Reusable Booster Family

Aegis is WSA's reusable booster family and the launcher behind many WSA missions. Guided by WSA AutoLand V2.3, an Aegis booster has now touched down directly on its designated landing pad. The newest design, Aegis Mk1.7, adds a hinge for WSA's future booster catch. The next step is making the landing repeatable and dependable.

Configurations and Versions

▼

Aegis configurations are named Quartet, Quintet, Nonet, Heavy, Super Heavy and Max. Engine counts and performance figures for each are not documented.

Versions named in WSA flight records include:

  • Aegis Mk1.3: launched the Minmus Firework mission.
  • Aegis-1 Super Heavy (AegisSH-1): launched FAMMOK-3, Project Kepler and a KGOSS-1 ISRU module.
  • Aegis-1.1 Super Heavy: launched Odysseus.

Landing and Recovery

▼

Aegis boosters come back under AutoLand, WSA's own kOS landing software, written instead of relying on MechJeb.

  • First target-pad landing: with AutoLand V2.3, an Aegis booster touched down directly on its designated landing pad. In the precision landing on video, an Aegis Mk1.7 came down on the pad's marked centre and stayed upright. Watch the landing.
  • How it got there: AutoLand V2.2's powered-glide guidance kept missing by more than 1.6 km until the team realised it was missing Kerbin's rotation. Accounting for it cut the miss to about 500 m, and V2.3 closed the rest.
  • Earlier recoveries: WSA's records count more than 47 successful recoveries, including water landings, though boosters sometimes tipped over after touchdown.
  • Super Heavy recovery tests: test flights of Super Heavy versions 1.1 to 1.7 brought boosters down on land and in the sea.
  • Next: repeatable, dependable landings across conditions and Aegis configurations, the goal of AutoLand V3.0.

Separate Recovery and Automation Projects

▼
  • Booster catch (in development): a mechanical structure to catch and secure returning boosters instead of landing them on legs or recovering them from the sea. After considering a capture ring and cable systems, WSA chose to pursue an original chopstick-style design. The new Aegis Mk1.7 adds a hinge for the catch, and more about it will be released soon. A catch has not been demonstrated yet. It is separate from AutoLand.
  • Aegis flight automation (concept): automated ascent, staging, boostback burns and custom recovery locations. These would be parts of a wider Aegis automation system; AutoLand's job is the powered descent and landing.
  • Mark II (planned): recovering side boosters.
  • Mark V (possible): recovering the main booster.

Failures and Lessons

▼
  • Tipping after touchdown: even AutoLand V1.6, the last stable version, could land a booster that then tipped over.
  • AutoLand B1: an experimental build that failed in testing.
  • Flipping near apogee: an Aegis stabilizer setup has flipped near apogee.
  • A persistent 1.6 km miss: AutoLand V2.2 kept missing by more than 1.6 km. That made the team suspicious, and they realised the guidance was missing Kerbin's rotation; accounting for it cut the miss to about 500 m.
  • Splashdown: an Aegis splashdown is what led to the catch-structure concept.

Lyra: Next-Generation Booster Family

Lyra is a newer, larger and more powerful reusable booster family, intended for future high-capacity missions and KGOSS construction. It is in development: no Lyra flight is recorded yet.

Tiers and Prototype

▼
  • Tiers: Selene (smallest), Sol (middle) and Astra (largest).
  • Lyra P-1E: Prototype 1 Experimental, the first Lyra prototype. A design exists; it has not flown.

Engine Concept

▼

The Lyra concept uses 21 main Vector engines, with up to 9 more on each side depending on the configuration. These are concept-level figures, not final production specifications; the actual design and thrust will be confirmed when the vehicle is built and tested.

Aircraft & Spaceplane Program

WSA builds aircraft and spaceplanes to try out flight-control ideas, high-speed flight and reusable transport to orbit. Many of the designs below are still undocumented.

Swift-1X Test Aircraft

▼

A stable, fast, small aircraft used for testing aircraft concepts. It can fly crewed or uncrewed.

Crew Return Craft

▼
  • Blue Whale 1 / BWP-1LC: a special crew return craft in the Atlas line, associated with rare pickup and recovery operations on Kerbin. One configuration carries six engineers with about 6,600 m/s of delta-v before RCS. No individual flights are documented yet.
  • Manta Ray (MRR-1XLR), "Starfield": a spaceplane photographed above the planet at sunrise. Its role and flights still need documenting.

Other Designations

▼
  • FSX series (FSX-1P, FSX-2, FSX-2S, FSX-3): FSX-3 has been flown to Mach 5.
  • FX series (FX-1 to FX-4), FAX series (FAX-1, FAX-2, FAX-3, FAX-3E, FAX-3C, FAX-3S) and SRX-1: named designs that are not documented yet.

SSTO Development

▼

Single-stage-to-orbit work asks whether a spaceplane can reach orbit, and KGOSS, without dropping stages.

  • Design: a Mk2-based spaceplane of about 80 tonnes in its expanded form, with four engines, two shock-cone and four radial intakes, four crew modules and about 800 units of monopropellant.
  • Earlier design: ran out of liquid fuel near 25 km.
  • Later design: with RAPIER and NERV engines, it reached KGOSS's orbit at about 200 km. Docking had never been done with it before and the risk of hitting the station's centrifuge was too great, so the docking was aborted, and the SSTO flew home and landed safely at KSC.
  • Lesson: reaching orbit is not the same as completing the mission. A single stage has to carry the fuel for the climb, the rendezvous, the docking and the trip home.
